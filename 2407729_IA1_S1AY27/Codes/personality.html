Personality, Mentor & Perspectives

Academic Approach • Steve Jobs • AI Tools in Education

This page outlines how I approach my coursework through three traits: analytical thinking, persistence, and attention to detail. It also covers why I look to Steve Jobs's work as a standard, followed by a short essay on when AI tools help student learning and when they become a crutch.

Top Three Personality Traits

Writing software requires patience and methodical thinking. In my computing coursework at UTech, three habits guide how I study and write code:

1. Analytical Thinking

When I run into a difficult programming problem, I break it down into smaller parts instead of guessing. I look at the inputs, edge cases, and algorithmic complexity. In classes like Web Programming and Discrete Mathematics, this keeps me calm when reading long assignment briefs or debugging errors.

2. Persistence

Coding assignments regularly break. Whether it is an unexpected layout bug in CSS or an issue with pointer arithmetic in C, errors take time to diagnose. Persistence means staying with the problem, stepping through each line, and treating every failed test as useful feedback rather than a reason to stop.

3. Precision

Details matter in software. Missing a closing tag in HTML, misspelling a CSS property, or ignoring an off-by-one error in a loop creates problems down the line. I take care with syntax, indentation, and semantic structure, which keeps code readable and makes validation errors rare.

Mentor Case Study: Steve Jobs

Background

Steve Jobs co-founded Apple in 1976, was ousted in 1985, ran NeXT and Pixar, and returned to Apple in 1997. Over the following decade and a half, he oversaw the development of the iMac, OS X, the iPod, the iPhone, and the iPad. His career demonstrates how product design, engineering, and typography can work together.

“Details matter, it's worth waiting to get it right.”

- Steve Jobs

Connection to My Traits

I look to Jobs's career because it shows how my three core traits apply to building technology:

  • Attention to Detail: Jobs famously insisted that the circuit boards inside the original Macintosh look neat, even though users would never open the case. That emphasis on care, whether visible to the customer or not, is how I try to treat backend logic and semantic markup.
  • Persistence: Being fired from the company he started would have ended most careers. Instead, he spent twelve years building NeXT and Pixar before returning to rescue Apple from near-bankruptcy. That resilience reminds me that setbacks in coursework or projects are part of the process.
  • Analytical Focus: Jobs was skilled at cutting unnecessary features to focus on what users actually needed. In software design, removing clutter is usually harder than adding more code.

Critical Essay: Using AI as a Tool Rather than a Crutch

Tool or Crutch: The Reality of Generative AI in Student Life

By Ajani E. Davy • CIT2011 Web Programming • University of Technology, Jamaica

Instructors often repeat the phrase: "LLMs such as ChatGPT, Claude, and CoPilot must be used as a tool, not a crutch." In principle, almost every student agrees. In practice, student life is complicated, and the line between an honest learning aid and a survival shortcut is easy to cross. Understanding when AI helps or hurts comes down to looking at where it genuinely teaches, why students reach for it under stress, and what boundaries keep learning intact.

When AI Serves as a Learning Aid

Used properly, a language model acts like a patient tutor available late at night (Mollick & Mollick, 2023). In computer science, beginners often get stuck on opaque compiler errors or subtle syntax rules. Asking an LLM to explain what a specific error message means, or having it generate test inputs to check an algorithm, saves hours of confusion without doing the thinking for you (Kasneci et al., 2023). It can also rephrase dense technical documentation into plain language or suggest two different ways to structure a data model so you can compare the trade-offs.

Why Students Rely on AI as a Crutch

Most students do not use AI out of laziness; they use it when they feel trapped. Mid-semester schedules often collide: three programming assignments, a group presentation, and two tests in the same week, alongside part-time jobs, commute times, or family responsibilities. When midnight arrives and an assignment is due in six hours, pasting a prompt into ChatGPT feels like the only way to avoid a failing grade (Cotton et al., 2023).

While understandable, this creates a real problem. Programming requires cognitive friction. The hours spent debugging a loop or tracing a pointer bug are when the concept actually takes root. When you outsource the writing and problem solving to an AI, you hand in working code, but your brain misses the practice. Come exam time, or during a technical job interview, that missing foundation shows immediately.

How Students Should Use LLMs

To keep AI as a tool rather than a crutch, I follow three practical guidelines:

  • Ask for Explanations, Not Solutions: Instead of prompting "Write the code for this assignment," ask "Explain how Flexbox space-between handles leftover margin" or "What edge cases should I test for a binary search?"
  • Use AI for Verification: Write your own logic first. Once it works, you can ask an AI to point out potential performance bottlenecks or overlooked edge cases.
  • The Whiteboard Test: Never submit code you cannot step through line by line on a whiteboard. If you cannot explain why a line exists, you have crossed the line from using a tool to leaning on a crutch.

Conclusion

Generative AI is a permanent part of software engineering. Learning to work with it is a useful skill, but leaning on it to skip the work of learning leaves you with a paper degree and no real capability. For students aiming for careers in software engineering, the only sustainable path is to use AI to clarify ideas, while keeping the actual thinking and coding your own.
